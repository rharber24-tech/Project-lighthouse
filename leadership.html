How I Lead

Four Principles Guide My Leadership

Effective learning leadership requires more than technical expertise. It requires the ability to develop people, manage complexity, make thoughtful decisions, and continuously improve the systems around them.

Lead People

Build trust, develop talent, create clarity, and give people the support and autonomy they need to do their best work.

Manage the Work

Translate strategy into priorities, timelines, workflows, and deliverables while keeping stakeholders aligned and projects moving.

Design With Purpose

Ground decisions in instructional design theory, learning science, accessibility, learner needs, and organizational goals.

Improve Systems

Standardize what should be consistent, improve what is inefficient, and create reusable systems that allow good work to scale.

My Leadership Story

From the Kitchen to the Classroom to Learning Leadership

I learned leadership in environments where preparation, people, quality, timing, resources, and execution all mattered. As an Executive Chef, I led teams through high-pressure operations while developing people, solving problems, and delivering experiences that had to work in the real world.

When I moved into instructional design and learning technology, I brought that same mindset with me. I learned to look beyond the individual course or learning object and consider the larger system: the people involved, the process behind the work, the technology supporting it, and the experience ultimately delivered to the learner.

Today, that perspective shapes how I approach instructional design leadership. I believe strong leaders create clarity, remove barriers, develop people, and make it easier for teams to produce meaningful work.

Project Leadership

Turning Complex Work Into Manageable Steps

I approach project management as a leadership responsibility—not simply a tracking exercise.

Define

Clarify the problem, desired outcomes, stakeholders, scope, and success criteria.

Plan

Establish priorities, dependencies, timelines, resources, and practical workflows.

Align

Keep teams and stakeholders informed, engaged, and moving toward the same outcome.

Execute

Remove barriers, monitor progress, solve problems, and keep deliverables moving.

Evaluate

Review outcomes, identify lessons, and determine what should change next time.

Improve

Turn lessons learned into better processes, standards, resources, and experiences.

Instructional Design Practice

Theory Should Influence the Design—Not Just the Vocabulary

Instructional design frameworks give me a structure for making better decisions. They are tools for solving learning problems, not simply concepts to list on a résumé.

Backward Design

Begin with the desired learning outcomes and work backward to determine evidence, activities, content, and experience.

ADDIE

Use analysis, design, development, implementation, and evaluation as a practical framework for iterative learning development.

Bloom's Taxonomy

Align learning activities and assessments with the level of thinking learners need to demonstrate.

Accessibility

Consider accessibility and usability from the beginning rather than treating them as final-stage corrections.

Continuous Improvement

Prepare. Execute. Learn. Improve.

I believe the best leaders are never finished learning. My "1% better" mindset means looking for small, meaningful improvements in how teams work, how systems operate, and how learners experience the results.

Leadership Philosophy

Develop People. Improve Systems. Create Better Experiences.

Leadership isn't about having all the answers. It's about creating the conditions for people and ideas to succeed—and having the discipline to keep improving what comes next.
