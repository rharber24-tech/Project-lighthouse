The Challenge

Exploring where AI can create value without replacing educators.

Artificial intelligence is rapidly changing the possibilities for learning and assessment. But the introduction of AI into education raises an important question: where can technology genuinely improve the experience?

Assessment can require significant time, particularly when instructors evaluate large numbers of learner submissions while attempting to provide timely, meaningful feedback.

The opportunity was to explore AI-enhanced workflows that could improve efficiency while keeping instructional judgement, learner experience, and academic quality at the center of the process.

Strategic Approach

Start with the problem. Then determine where AI belongs.

Rather than adopting AI simply because the technology was available, the approach focused on identifying meaningful opportunities within existing instructional and assessment workflows.

Identify the Opportunity

Examined assessment workflows to identify repetitive tasks and opportunities where AI could potentially reduce administrative burden without reducing instructional quality.

Design the Workflow

Considered how AI could fit into an existing instructional workflow rather than treating technology as a replacement for the educator.

Protect Human Judgement

Kept instructor oversight and meaningful evaluation at the center of the process, recognizing that assessment involves more than simply producing a score.

Evaluate Responsibly

Considered accuracy, consistency, transparency, learner experience, and appropriate human oversight when evaluating potential AI workflows.

Responsible AI

Innovation requires intentional guardrails.

Effective AI adoption in education requires more than technical capability. It requires clear expectations around quality, transparency, responsibility, and human oversight.

Human in the Loop

AI should support instructional expertise, not eliminate the professional judgement of educators.

Quality Matters

Efficiency only creates value when the resulting learning experience maintains appropriate quality and instructional integrity.

Transparency

Learners and educators should understand how technology participates in an assessment or learning workflow.

Learner-Centered

The measure of successful innovation is not the sophistication of the technology—it is whether learners and educators are better supported.

Instructional Design Lens

AI should make good instructional design stronger.

AI does not replace instructional design. It creates new possibilities for how instructional designers approach repetitive tasks, content development, feedback, and learner support.

The same principles that guide effective learning design still matter: clear objectives, meaningful practice, appropriate cognitive demand, accessibility, feedback, and alignment between learning activities and outcomes.

The technology becomes valuable when it supports those principles rather than distracting from them.

Leadership Takeaway

The goal isn't to use AI. The goal is to use it well.

Effective innovation starts with a meaningful problem, not a new technology. My approach to AI focuses on understanding the workflow, protecting the learner experience, supporting educators, and determining where technology can create genuine value.
